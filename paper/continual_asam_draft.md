# Continual ASAM Paper Draft

This file is a paper-ready writing scaffold for the continual-learning extension of ASAM. It is intentionally written as a standalone draft instead of patching `paper/asam_paper.tex`, so the original long-sequence paper remains intact.

## Working Title

**Continual ASAM: Prototype-Routed Sparse Attention with Capacity-Constrained Optimal Transport and Transport-Aware Lifecycle Control**

## Abstract

Continual learning with Transformer-style attention suffers from a structural tension between plasticity and interference: new tasks require routing flexibility, while previously learned tasks require stable reuse of attention structure. Existing sparse-attention methods mainly target computational efficiency, and existing continual-learning routing methods typically do not expose an explicit sparse-attention geometry that can be diagnosed and controlled across task stages. We present **Continual ASAM**, a continual-learning extension of adaptive sparse attention in which sparse pattern selection is mediated by learned prototype routing rather than only by task identifiers. The method combines four ingredients inside one sparse-attention controller: (1) prototype-routed sparse attention-pattern selection, (2) capacity-constrained Sinkhorn routing that defines prototype usage and deficit statistics, (3) transport-aware prototype lifecycle updates including split, merge, reset, and relocation, and (4) a closed diagnostic loop that exports transport loss, routing drift, and excess statistics for online adaptation. We show that the resulting controller admits a coherent continual-learning surrogate interpretation: transport loss controls barycenter drift, routing KL controls route drift relative to remembered behavior, and prototype excess measures routing interference above the uniform-collision floor. Empirically, the repository provides runnable synthetic and real-text continual-learning pipelines, multi-seed ablations, and stage-wise diagnostic reports that connect these geometric quantities to forgetting. Our claim is not the invention of sparse attention, Sinkhorn routing, or prototype memory in isolation, but the integration of these mechanisms into a unified continual sparse-routing framework with explicit mathematical control variables.

## Claimed Contributions

1. **Prototype-routed continual sparse attention.** We extend ASAM from adaptive sparse attention for efficiency to a continual-learning setting in which sparse attention-pattern selection is controlled by prototype routing.
2. **Capacity-constrained OT control variables.** We use Sinkhorn routing not only to balance assignments, but to define prototype capacity targets and excess statistics that become explicit continual-learning state variables.
3. **Transport-aware lifecycle updates.** We implement split, merge, reset, and relocation rules driven by latent barycenters, support statistics, and transport excess rather than by purely heuristic reinitialization.
4. **Theory-to-system alignment.** The quantities used in the mathematical argument (`transport_loss`, routing KL, excess, merge/split counts) are computed in the model, optimized during training, exported by the benchmark, and reused by the online controller.
5. **Executable continual-learning benchmark suite.** We provide synthetic continual training, a real-text benchmark, multi-seed ablations, and report generation utilities that expose stage-wise forgetting together with routing geometry diagnostics.

## Introduction Draft

Transformer-based architectures remain difficult to deploy in continual-learning settings because parameter sharing alone does not control *where* interference occurs. In standard attention, all tasks compete over the same dense interaction graph, and even efficient sparse-attention variants usually focus on reducing computational cost rather than separating task-specific routes through attention space. As a result, the question of how sparse attention should be structured for continual learning remains underexplored: efficiency-oriented sparsity does not automatically yield stability, and replay or regularization methods do not directly expose a routing geometry that can be measured or controlled.

We take the view that continual learning should be enforced at the level of sparse routing structure. Instead of treating sparsity as a static computational pattern, we let the model choose sparse attention supports through prototype routing. Each input sequence is first mapped to a sparse mixture over learned prototypes, and that prototype mixture in turn predicts which attention patterns and heads should be active. This makes sparse attention analyzable through routing geometry: routing concentration, route drift, capacity imbalance, and prototype redundancy become measurable objects rather than implicit side effects of optimization.

To stabilize this routing geometry across tasks, we introduce a capacity-constrained optimal-transport step over prototype assignments. The purpose of this step is not merely to balance routing loads, but to define a continual-learning state through prototype usage, capacity, support, and excess statistics. These statistics enable transport-aware lifecycle control: overloaded prototypes can be split toward deficit barycenters, redundant prototypes can be merged and recycled, and low-utility prototypes can be reset toward under-served regions rather than random directions. In parallel, routing KL against remembered priors and transport losses provide differentiable surrogates for route stability and geometric drift.

The resulting method, which we call **Continual ASAM**, is best understood as a framework-level contribution. We do not claim to invent sparse attention, Sinkhorn routing, prototype memory, or continual replay independently. Instead, our contribution is the unification of these ingredients into a single continual sparse-attention controller with explicit mathematical control variables and an executable evaluation pipeline. This framing is important both technically and rhetorically: technically because it aligns optimization, lifecycle control, and diagnostics around the same quantities, and rhetorically because it yields a more defensible novelty claim than presenting each component as individually new.

## Method Overview Draft

### Problem Setup

We consider class-incremental or stage-wise continual learning over a sequence of tasks `D_1, D_2, ..., D_T`. The goal is to learn a shared sparse-attention backbone that can absorb new tasks while preserving useful routing structure for previously seen tasks.

### Prototype-Routed Sparse Attention

Given an input sequence representation `x`, the model computes a normalized latent `z(x)` and routes it to a sparse distribution `p(x)` over learned prototype anchors `{a_i}`. This routing distribution does not directly produce logits; instead, it controls sparse attention by selecting pattern mixtures and head importances. Sparse attention therefore becomes prototype-mediated rather than only task-conditioned.

### Capacity-Constrained OT Routing

Routing is biased by a remembered prior and then regularized through a Sinkhorn assignment with a capacity target. This produces two effects simultaneously: it discourages prototype collapse, and it defines explicit state variables `u`, `c`, and `e = u - c` that summarize routing usage, target capacity, and imbalance. These state variables are later reused by the lifecycle controller.

### Continual Surrogate

The continual-learning interpretation is built around three principal quantities:

- `transport_loss`, which limits how far the routed prototype barycenter can move away from the latent geometry,
- `routing_stability_loss`, which limits route drift relative to remembered routing behavior,
- `||excess||^2`, which measures routing concentration above the uniform-collision floor.

This yields a practical surrogate view of forgetting: forgetting is controlled not directly, but through geometric drift, routing drift, and prototype interference.

### Transport-Aware Lifecycle Control

Prototype memories are updated online, and lifecycle operations are triggered from persistent usage, support, capacity, and excess statistics. Split operations move mass from overloaded prototypes toward deficit regions; merge operations recycle redundant prototypes; reset operations recover nearly unused prototypes. The key design choice is that these operations are driven by transport-aware statistics rather than by arbitrary periodic refresh rules.

### Closed Diagnostic Loop

A central design principle of Continual ASAM is that the theory variables are not only explanatory notation. The implementation exports the same variables that appear in the mathematical argument, including transport loss, routing stability, excess statistics, and merge/split counts. The benchmark then correlates these quantities with forgetting and feeds them into an online hyperparameter controller.

## Related Work Draft

Sparse attention, routing, and continual learning have each been studied extensively, but usually in partial isolation. Existing sparse-attention methods such as local-window, strided, random, and clustered attention primarily target computational efficiency and long-context modeling. Their main objective is to reduce the quadratic cost of dense attention, not to define a continual-learning routing geometry that can be tracked across task stages. In parallel, continual-learning routing methods study task-conditioned path selection or modular reuse, but they typically do not expose the sparse attention support itself as the object being routed and diagnosed. Our work sits at the intersection of these directions: it treats sparse attention not only as an efficiency mechanism, but also as a controllable route for mitigating interference.

Prototype-based continual learning provides a second relevant thread. Prototype memories, prototype replay, and class-summary methods aim to preserve task information compactly across stages. However, in most of these methods prototypes summarize representations or replay targets; they do not directly control sparse attention-pattern selection. Continual ASAM instead uses prototype routing as the mechanism that decides which sparse supports and heads are active, thereby turning prototype geometry into an explicit control surface for attention reuse and separation.

A third closely related line of work comes from optimal transport and sparse routing. Sinkhorn-based balancing, differentiable top-`k` assignment, and load-aware routing are known tools in sparse expert systems and differentiable selection operators. Our use of optimal transport is motivated differently: we use capacity-constrained routing not only to balance assignments, but to define stage-persistent state variables---usage, capacity, support, and excess---that can drive continual-learning lifecycle control. This is the main distinction from efficiency-only or balancing-only routing formulations.

Taken together, prior work motivates each ingredient of Continual ASAM, but not their exact combination. The closest prior directions justify why prototypes, routing, sparse supports, and OT balancing are individually reasonable; our contribution is to unify them into a continual sparse-attention controller whose theory variables are directly observable in code and benchmark outputs.

## Formal Method Draft

### Setup

We consider a sequence of tasks `D_1, D_2, ..., D_T`. At task stage `t`, the model receives examples from `D_t` and updates a shared sparse-attention backbone. The objective is to absorb new tasks while preserving routing structure that remains useful for previously seen tasks.

### Prototype-routed sparse attention

Given a sequence representation `x`, the model computes a normalized latent `z(x)` and a sparse routing distribution `p(x)` over `P` learned prototype anchors `{a_i}_{i=1}^P`. The routing distribution is not used directly for prediction. Instead, the prototype mixture is fed to a sparse gate that predicts (i) per-head sparse-pattern weights and (ii) head-importance scores. Sparse attention is then computed only on the support induced by the selected patterns. This makes sparse attention prototype-mediated rather than only task-mediated.

### Capacity-constrained routing

Routing is biased by a remembered prior and then projected through a capacity-constrained Sinkhorn step. The resulting dense transport plan is finally projected to a sparse top-`k` support. This design serves two purposes. First, it keeps routing computationally sparse. Second, it defines explicit state variables for continual learning: average usage `u`, target capacity `c`, support statistics, and the excess vector `e = u - c`.

### Continual surrogate

The continual-learning controller is organized around the surrogate

`Psi = lambda_T E[T(x)] + lambda_R E[R(x)] + lambda_E ||e||_2^2 + lambda_D D(A)`

where `T(x)` is transport loss, `R(x)` is routing KL against the remembered prior, `e` is prototype excess, and `D(A)` is prototype redundancy. The role of this surrogate is not to replace task loss, but to provide geometric control variables that can be optimized jointly with task supervision and inspected stage-by-stage.

### Prototype lifecycle control

After each task stage, prototype statistics are updated through exponential moving averages. Lifecycle operators are then applied using those statistics. Split operations allocate new support to under-served regions by moving mass from overloaded prototypes toward deficit barycenters. Merge operations collapse redundant prototypes and recycle freed capacity. Reset operations recover nearly unused prototypes. These rules are transport-aware because relocation is guided by latent barycenters and excess/deficit structure rather than random reinitialization alone.

### Diagnostic feedback loop

A distinctive design choice in Continual ASAM is that the theory variables are exported as benchmark signals. Transport loss, routing stability, excess statistics, and merge/split counts are recorded after each stage and correlated with forgetting. The online controller can then adapt prototype hyperparameters from those diagnostics, turning continual-learning geometry into a feedback signal rather than a passive explanation.

### Why this formulation is useful

The method is useful because it turns three informal continual-learning questions into explicit measurable objects:

1. **How far did the new route drift?** Controlled by routing KL and remembered priors.
2. **How far did the routed representation move away from the latent geometry?** Controlled by transport loss.
3. **How concentrated and interfering did routing become?** Controlled by the excess vector and its derived collision interpretation.

This makes the method easier to analyze, ablate, and adapt than a sparse-attention system whose continual-learning behavior is only inferred from final accuracy.
## Experiments Draft

We evaluate Continual ASAM at three complementary levels so that the empirical story matches the mathematical one. First, `experiments/train_continual_asam.py` provides a controlled synthetic continual-learning scaffold for mechanism checks, rapid debugging, and stage-wise inspection of routing variables under clean task shifts. Second, `experiments/run_continual_text_benchmark.py` provides the main real-text continual benchmark on class-incremental Split AG News, where the method is tested under a natural-language workload rather than only on synthetic token structure. Third, `experiments/run_continual_text_ablation.py` wraps the real-text benchmark into a multi-seed comparison across routing and adaptation strategies, which is the most appropriate source for paper tables because it reports aggregated means and standard deviations rather than a single illustrative run.

The current experimental protocol should be described conservatively and reproducibly. All compared methods share the same backbone width, number of heads, layer count, top-`k` attention-pattern budget, optimizer family, replay policy, and task order; the intended difference is the sparse-routing controller rather than unrelated optimization changes. In the present runnable suite, the benchmark comparison is centered on four strategies already implemented in code: `task_routing`, `no_adaptation`, `correlation`, and `meta_secant`. The first is the explicit task-conditioned continual baseline, the second uses prototype routing without online updates, the third adds diagnostic correlation-based adaptation, and the fourth uses the secant-style meta controller. This means the current paper evidence is strongest on the question of whether prototype routing plus adaptive control helps continual retention, not yet on every possible operator-level design choice. In the latest real-data suite currently checked into the repository, we ran a modest but non-trivial Split AG News configuration with `max_train_samples=256`, `max_val_samples=128`, and `num_seeds=3` for the ablations; this is the most appropriate current reference point for paper text because it is stronger than the smoke-test setup while still CPU-runnable.

Our primary task metrics are average final accuracy, average forgetting, and backward transfer, exported directly by the benchmark JSON. Because the proposed method is geometry-driven, we also report the internal quantities that the theory claims should matter: stage-wise transport gap, stage-wise transport loss, routing-stability loss, routing entropy, mean absolute excess, prototype lifecycle counts, and the per-stage optimization terms collected in `stage_training_metrics`. This is important for rigor. A continual sparse-attention method should not be judged only by endpoint accuracy; it should also demonstrate that the controller variables intended to regulate interference actually move in meaningful directions over time.

In the paper, the main experimental presentation should therefore be split into two tiers. The first tier is the multi-seed comparative table from the ablation runner, which answers the headline question of which routing/controller strategy achieves the best accuracy-forgetting trade-off. The second tier is a diagnostic case study from the detailed benchmark report, which visualizes the accuracy matrix, final-task accuracy profile, stage-wise theory traces, and task-prototype occupancy heatmap. This separation keeps the claims honest: the ablation gives the comparative evidence, while the benchmark report explains *why* a stronger strategy may be stronger.

For reproducibility, the one-command script `scripts/run_continual_paper_suite.py` already materializes the paper-facing artifacts. A standard run writes `continual_benchmark.json`, `continual_benchmark_plots.png`, `continual_benchmark_report.md`, `continual_ablation.json`, `continual_ablation.csv`, `continual_ablation_table.md`, `continual_ablation.png`, `continual_ablation_report.md`, `continual_operator_ablation.json`, `continual_operator_ablation.csv`, `continual_operator_ablation_table.md`, `continual_operator_ablation.png`, and `continual_operator_ablation_report.md`, together with a suite manifest and summary report. This makes it possible to draft the paper tables and figures directly from exported files instead of hand-copying transient notebook outputs.

## Ablation Draft

The current executable ablation should be framed as a **routing-and-controller ablation**, because that is what the repository presently supports end to end. Concretely, the ablation runner compares four strategies: `task_routing`, `no_adaptation`, `correlation`, and `meta_secant`. This decomposition already answers an important continual-learning question. It separates the benefit of explicit task routing from the benefit of prototype routing, and then separates the benefit of fixed prototype routing from the benefit of online diagnostic adaptation.

The ablation metrics should be reported exactly as the code exports them. For each strategy and seed, the runner summarizes `avg_accuracy`, `avg_forgetting`, `backward_transfer`, `final_stage_forgetting`, `final_transport_gap`, and `final_routing_stability`. These per-run summaries are then aggregated into mean and standard-deviation estimates across seeds, yielding the columns already written to `continual_ablation.csv` and `continual_ablation_table.md`. This is a strength of the current setup: the paper table can be lifted directly from repository artifacts, which reduces the risk of mismatched numbers between prose, tables, and JSON outputs. In the current `256/128, 3 seeds` run, `task_routing` is the strongest strategy-level baseline with `0.5234 mean / 0.0447 std` average accuracy and `-0.0469 mean / 0.0776 std` average forgetting, whereas all three prototype-routing strategies cluster around `0.4974 mean / 0.0295 std` average accuracy and `0.0469 mean / 0.0837 std` forgetting. This is an important negative result: at the present scale and training budget, prototype routing has not yet surpassed the explicit task-conditioned controller.

The interpretation of the ablation should also remain geometry-aware. If a strategy improves retention, the paper should check whether that gain coincides with a lower final transport gap, lower final routing drift, or more stable prototype usage rather than reporting an isolated accuracy improvement. For representative runs, the appendix can additionally pull `adaptation_steps`, `total_resets`, `total_splits`, and `final_hyperparameters` from the raw per-run summaries to show that the controller is changing the system's behavior in a measurable way instead of acting as a nominal switch.

Equally important is clarity about what the current ablation does **not** yet establish. The repository does not yet provide a full operator-by-operator toggle study for Sinkhorn removal, merge-only versus split-only lifecycle rules, reset-off settings, or task-free streams without stage boundaries. Those are valuable next experiments, but they should be positioned as future extensions rather than implied completed evidence. Presenting the current ablation in this narrower and more faithful way strengthens the credibility of the overall paper.

## Limitations and Future Work Draft

Continual ASAM still has several important limitations. First, the current mathematical story is a surrogate-control argument rather than a full forgetting theorem. The draft can justify transport loss, routing KL, excess statistics, and lifecycle events as meaningful variables under explicit assumptions, but it does not yet prove an assumption-free bound from those quantities to downstream test accuracy. Theoretical claims should therefore remain at the level of coherence, interpretability, and diagnostic usefulness rather than full optimality or convergence guarantees.

Second, the empirical scope is still benchmark-scale rather than foundation-model-scale. The synthetic benchmark is useful for controlled inspection, and Split AG News is a meaningful real-text continual setting, but the current runnable experiments use relatively small backbones and clear stage boundaries. This is enough to study routing geometry, yet it does not establish effectiveness for long task streams, larger pretrained language models, or more realistic non-stationary settings in which task identities are noisy or unavailable.

Third, the present ablation scope is narrower than the full design space discussed in the method. What is executable today is a clean routing/controller comparison, not a complete decomposition of every operator in the lifecycle mechanism. In particular, the paper should not overclaim evidence for the individual causal roles of Sinkhorn balancing, merge thresholds, relocation strength, or split/reset rules unless separate toggled experiments are added. A strong next step is to extend the ablation suite so each operator can be removed or isolated under the same benchmark protocol.

Fourth, the controller introduces real algorithmic overhead and bookkeeping complexity. Capacity-constrained routing, lifecycle maintenance, hyperparameter adaptation, and stage-wise diagnostic export all add state beyond a standard sparse-attention layer. For a continual-learning paper this trade-off may be acceptable, because the goal is retention and interpretability rather than raw throughput alone, but it remains important to quantify the overhead and to study whether some of the control variables can be simplified, tied together, or learned more automatically.

Finally, the novelty claim must remain appropriately scoped. Sparse attention, Sinkhorn routing, prototype memories, and continual-learning regularization all have substantial prior art. The contribution here is strongest as a framework-level integration claim: Continual ASAM turns these ingredients into one coherent sparse-routing system with exported geometry diagnostics and controllable lifecycle updates. Future work should test whether that integration principle transfers beyond continual text classification to broader modular routing settings such as sparse expert systems, adaptive memory controllers, or online multi-domain adaptation.

## Implementation Details Draft

Our implementation is intentionally lightweight so that the continual-routing effects remain interpretable. The reference classifier in `experiments/train_continual_asam.py` uses a token embedding layer, a learned positional embedding, a small stack of continual ASAM layers, layer normalization, sequence mean pooling, and a task-specific linear classifier head. In the present benchmark code, the real-text setting defaults to a compact backbone with `dim=64`, `num_heads=4`, `num_layers=1`, `top_k_patterns=2`, and AdamW with learning rate `3e-4`. This keeps the benchmark focused on routing behavior rather than on large-model scale.

The continual sparse-routing layer is configured through `asam/continual_asam.py`. The pattern bank currently contains four sparse attention operators: `local`, `strided`, `random`, and `hierarchical`. In `routing_mode="task"`, the model uses task-conditioned continual ASAM layers. In `routing_mode="prototype"`, the model swaps in `PrototypeContinualASAMLayer`, which replaces explicit task routing with learned prototype routing and maintains prototype memories, usage EMAs, capacity EMAs, support EMAs, excess EMAs, and latent prototype statistics. In the current text classifier, the number of prototypes is set to the number of tasks, which is a reasonable benchmark default but should be treated as a design choice rather than a theoretically required constraint.

Prototype routing is currently instantiated with a Sinkhorn-style capacity-aware controller. The default continual configuration uses `prototype_routing_strategy="sinkhorn_topk"`, `prototype_top_k=2`, `routing_temperature=1.0`, `prototype_prior_strength=1.0`, `prototype_prior_floor=1e-3`, `prototype_sinkhorn_epsilon=0.1`, `prototype_sinkhorn_iters=20`, and `prototype_capacity_blend=0.5`. Lifecycle-related defaults include `prototype_relocation_strength=0.75`, `prototype_reset_threshold=0.01`, `prototype_split_threshold=0.20`, `prototype_noise_scale=0.05`, `prototype_merge_threshold=0.9`, and `prototype_merge_usage_threshold=0.1` in the real-text benchmark configuration. These values should be reported explicitly because they determine when prototypes are refreshed, split, merged, or softly relocated after each stage.

Training combines task supervision with routing regularization. During each optimization step, the loss is the sum of cross-entropy and weighted auxiliary terms for overlap control, stability, balance, diversity, and transport consistency. In the current benchmark defaults, the weights are `overlap_weight=0.1`, `stability_weight=0.1`, `balance_weight=0.05`, `diversity_weight=0.05`, and `transport_weight=0.05`. The training code also records per-stage averages of `train_loss`, `overlap_loss`, `stability_loss`, `balance_loss`, `diversity_loss`, `transport_loss`, and `routing_stability_loss`, which makes it possible to analyze optimization behavior alongside final continual-learning accuracy.

Replay is implemented as a simple episodic buffer rather than a large external memory system. In the real-text benchmark, a replay buffer stores previous batches and reuses them with `replay_batch_size=4`; in the synthetic scaffold, replay is organized per task. This is a pragmatic choice: the current paper is not about replacing replay, but about testing whether continual sparse routing remains useful under a modest and controlled replay regime.

The adaptive controller is only active in prototype-routing runs when `adaptive_hyperparameters=True`. The benchmark currently supports two update modes, `correlation` and `meta_secant`, after an `adaptation_warmup_stages=1` delay. The adaptation logic exposes several explicit step sizes and meta-objective weights, including `prior_strength_step=0.25`, `capacity_blend_step=0.10`, `relocation_strength_step=0.10`, `meta_transport_weight=0.5`, `meta_transport_loss_weight=0.25`, `meta_excess_weight=0.25`, `meta_routing_weight=0.25`, `meta_secant_mix=0.7`, and `meta_secant_eps=1e-3`. We recommend reporting these values in the appendix because they define how aggressively the controller responds to rising transport loss, excess, or route drift.

For data handling, the current real-text pipeline uses class-incremental Split AG News with `classes_per_task=2` and `batch_size=8`. The default benchmark entry point remains intentionally lightweight, but the latest stronger paper-oriented run in this repository uses `max_length=128`, `max_train_samples=256`, `max_val_samples=128`, and `num_seeds=3` for the ablations. Even this stronger setting is still a small-scale CPU configuration rather than a final large-model benchmark, so the resulting numbers should be interpreted as a credible intermediate evaluation rather than a definitive scale claim. If stronger empirical claims are desired, future paper versions should rerun the same protocol with larger sample budgets, additional seeds, and stronger pretrained text encoders while keeping the routing/controller definitions fixed.

## Efficiency and Overhead Analysis Draft

Continual ASAM should be analyzed against two reference points: dense attention and task-conditioned continual ASAM. Relative to dense attention, the dominant attention term remains sparse support evaluation rather than prototype control. If each query-head attends to an average of `s̄` support positions after combining the selected sparse patterns, then the attention multiply is approximately `O(B H L s̄ d_h)` instead of dense `O(B H L^2 d_h)`, while the standard projection terms for `q`, `k`, `v`, and the output projection remain of the same order as in a standard Transformer block. In that sense, the continual extension does not remove the basic asymptotic advantage of sparse attention.

Relative to `ContinualASAMLayer`, however, `PrototypeContinualASAMLayer` introduces several additive controller costs. First, the layer pools each sequence, projects it into prototype space, and computes similarities against `M` prototype embeddings, which contributes approximately `O(B D E + B M E)` work when `D` is the model width and `E` is the prototype embedding dimension. Second, Sinkhorn routing adds an iterative balancing cost of approximately `O(I B M)`, where `I` is the number of Sinkhorn iterations, because each iteration alternates row and column renormalization over a `B × M` routing matrix. Third, prototype aggregation adds another `O(B M E)` term through the weighted combination of prototype embeddings. Finally, prototype-conditioned pattern and head prediction adds a small multilayer-perceptron overhead of order `O(B h (H P + H))`, where `h` is the controller hidden width and `P` is the number of sparse patterns. For the current benchmark defaults, `M`, `P`, and `H` are all small, so these controller terms are best understood as lower-order overheads on top of sparse attention rather than a new dominant cost.

The parameter and state overhead are also easy to characterize. Task-conditioned continual ASAM stores task memories of order `O(T (H + H P))`, where `T` is the number of tasks. Prototype routing instead stores prototype embeddings, latent prototype EMAs, head memories, pattern memories, and scalar usage/capacity/support/excess traces, giving memory of order `O(M (2 E + H + H P + 4))` per layer up to constant factors. This is still linear in the number of routing slots, but it is unavoidably larger than the task-conditioned version because the controller maintains a richer continual state.

A more important systems issue is that the current implementation is algorithmically sparse but not yet kernel-optimal. The continual layer caches base pattern masks as dense tensors of shape `[P, H, L, L]`, selects top-`k` pattern unions in dense mask space, and only then converts the selected support into gather indices for sparse attention. As a result, the final attention multiply is sparse, but support construction still carries dense `O(P H L^2)` mask storage and temporary mask-manipulation costs. This is an honest but important qualification: the present code should be described as a sparse-support operator with dense mask bookkeeping, not yet as a fully fused sparse kernel. For short and medium sequences this is acceptable, but at larger `L` the dense mask cache and per-sample mask assembly become the main implementation bottlenecks.

There is also a second category of overhead that appears only at stage boundaries. Prototype lifecycle maintenance is invoked through `refresh_prototypes()` once per task stage rather than once per minibatch, so its cost is amortized across the entire stage. Split and reset logic are roughly linear in the number of prototypes, but merge selection computes pairwise similarities among seen prototype anchors and therefore has worst-case cost on the order of `O(M^2 E)`. The hyperparameter adaptation controller and diagnostic export operate only on short stage-wise traces and are correspondingly lightweight relative to repeated forward/backward passes. For continual learning, this distinction matters: most of the extra control overhead is not paid at every token interaction, but at task-transition times.

The practical efficiency story is therefore regime dependent. When sequence length is moderate to large and the number of prototypes remains small, the additive controller overhead is typically lower order than sparse attention itself, so Continual ASAM should retain the main asymptotic motivation of sparse routing. When sequence length is short, or when the number of prototypes grows with the number of tasks, the routing controller becomes more visible in wall-clock time. When sequence length becomes very large, the current dense mask cache and mask-construction path become the limiting factor, even though the final attention computation still uses sparse supports.

For this reason, a fair empirical efficiency section should separate model-compute savings from controller overhead. We recommend reporting at least four measurements: per-step training time, peak memory, stage-end prototype-refresh time, and inference latency with `return_info=False` so diagnostic export is disabled. The comparison should include dense attention, original ASAM, task-conditioned continual ASAM, and prototype-routed continual ASAM under the same width, depth, batch size, and sequence length. Until those measurements are run, the safest claim is that Continual ASAM preserves sparse-attention asymptotics up to additive routing, lifecycle, and bookkeeping overheads; the current implementation should not yet be presented as a throughput-optimized sparse kernel.
## Reproducibility Checklist Draft

The current repository already supports a reasonably strong reproducibility story, but it should be presented with clear scope. Code for the continual-learning extension, synthetic training scaffold, real-text benchmark, ablation runner, and one-command paper suite is contained inside the repository and can be executed from scripts rather than reconstructed from notebook fragments. In particular, `experiments/train_continual_asam.py`, `experiments/run_continual_text_benchmark.py`, `experiments/run_continual_text_ablation.py`, and `scripts/run_continual_paper_suite.py` together define the end-to-end evaluation pipeline.

A paper-facing reproducibility checklist should explicitly state the following items.

- **Code availability.** The method implementation, benchmark scripts, report generators, and tests are all present in the public codebase rather than being hidden in supplementary-only assets.
- **Environment specification.** The repository declares Python support from `3.8` upward and provides lower-bounded dependencies in `requirements.txt`, including PyTorch, NumPy, Matplotlib, and PyTest. This is sufficient for a runnable baseline, but not yet a fully pinned environment; future releases should add an exact lockfile or container specification.
- **Determinism controls.** The benchmark and training scripts expose explicit random seeds, and the real-text ablation runner evaluates multiple seeds per strategy. Final paper tables should report the exact seed list used for each experiment.
- **Model configuration.** The key architectural controls are already exposed as script arguments, including model width, heads, layers, top-`k` pattern budget, routing mode, prototype thresholds, replay batch size, and adaptation settings. The appendix should include the exact command lines or dumped config dictionaries for every main table and figure.
- **Data protocol.** The current real-text experiments use class-incremental Split AG News with configurable sample caps, sequence length, batch size, and classes per task. The paper should specify the task split, tokenizer/input preprocessing path, training/validation sample caps, and any filtering rules so that the dataset stream can be reconstructed exactly.
- **Evaluation metrics.** The benchmark exports `avg_accuracy`, `avg_forgetting`, `backward_transfer`, `accuracy_matrix`, stage-wise training metrics, lifecycle summaries, and theory diagnostics. The paper should define these metrics in the main text and confirm that all reported scalar values are computed from exported JSON artifacts rather than by manual spreadsheet edits.
- **Artifact release.** The paper suite already writes JSON summaries, Markdown reports, CSV tables, and PNG plots. Releasing these generated artifacts alongside the final version of the code would materially strengthen reproducibility because readers could compare their reruns against known-good outputs.
- **Statistical reporting.** The ablation runner aggregates mean and standard deviation across seeds, which is a good default. The final paper should report the number of seeds for every table, avoid single-seed headline claims, and distinguish exploratory diagnostics from multi-seed comparisons.
- **Hardware disclosure.** The scripts support CPU execution and expose a `device` argument, but the repository does not yet enforce a hardware-locked benchmark configuration. A complete paper version should report the hardware used for all timing and memory results, especially in the efficiency section.
- **Known gaps.** The current codebase does not yet provide a fully pinned environment, a large-scale pretrained backbone benchmark, or a full operator-toggle ablation suite. Including these limitations directly in the reproducibility checklist improves credibility rather than weakening it.

A concise paper version of this checklist can close with a simple rule: every number in the main tables should be traceable to a saved JSON, CSV, or Markdown report generated by the released scripts, and every figure should be reproducible from the same command-line pipeline. This is already close to true for the present repository and is one of the strongest aspects of the current project.

## Extended Operator Ablations Draft

The next ablation layer should move beyond strategy-level comparisons and isolate the operator-level mechanisms that are meant to deliver continual-learning gains. The central question is no longer simply whether prototype routing helps, but *which operator inside prototype-routed continual ASAM is responsible for the gain, and through which diagnostic variable does that gain appear?* This is the most important remaining experimental section if the paper aims to make a mathematically grounded claim rather than only a system-integration claim.

A useful way to organize this section is around four operator families: routing, transport regularization, lifecycle control, and online adaptation. Each family corresponds to a specific mathematical object in the theory and a specific implementation hook in the code.

First, **routing-operator ablations** should compare capacity-aware prototype routing against simpler sparse routing. The code already supports `sinkhorn_topk` and `kl_topk` routing strategies inside `PrototypeSparseGate`, although the benchmark CLI does not yet expose this switch directly. This ablation is mathematically important because Sinkhorn routing is the mechanism that turns prototype usage into a capacity-constrained transport object rather than a plain sparse assignment. If the Sinkhorn formulation is useful, replacing it with KL-biased top-`k` routing should typically increase transport imbalance, weaken the meaning of excess statistics, and make prototype occupancy more collapse-prone even when end-task accuracy changes only modestly.

Second, **transport-objective ablations** should isolate the role of `transport_loss` as a barycenter-drift surrogate. This is already easy to support because training scripts expose `transport_weight`. A minimal operator study should compare `transport_weight=0` against the default transport-aware setting. Under the current mathematical story, removing this term should not necessarily destroy performance immediately, but it should weaken alignment between routed latent geometry and prototype barycenters, increase stage transport-loss traces, and reduce the interpretability of transport-diagnostic correlations with forgetting.

Third, **lifecycle ablations** should decompose split, merge, reset, and relocation. The current implementation already exposes enough threshold controls to make this practical. In particular, split can be effectively suppressed by using a very high `prototype_split_threshold`; reset can be suppressed with `prototype_reset_threshold=0`; merge can be effectively disabled by setting `prototype_merge_usage_threshold=0`; and relocation can be neutralized with `prototype_relocation_strength=0`. This yields a clean ladder of experiments: no lifecycle moves, split-only, split-plus-reset, split-plus-reset-plus-merge, and the full transport-aware controller with relocation. The expected diagnostic signatures are different. Removing split should increase positive excess and overused prototypes; removing reset should leave low-support prototypes idle for longer; removing merge should increase redundancy and reduce capacity recycling; removing relocation should weaken transport-gap recovery after stage transitions.

Fourth, **closed-loop adaptation ablations** should separate diagnostics as measurements from diagnostics as control signals. The repository already supports `adaptive_hyperparameters=False`, `adaptation_strategy="correlation"`, and `adaptation_strategy="meta_secant"`, so the paper can compare open-loop prototype routing, a first-order diagnostic controller, and the secant-style controller. This ablation is especially important for the mathematical framing: if the proposed theory variables are meaningful control variables rather than post-hoc narratives, then enabling adaptation should improve the accuracy-forgetting trade-off *through* changes in transport gap, transport loss, routing stability, and excess, not just through unexplained accuracy movement.

For clarity, the operator-ablation section should report three groups of outputs for every setting. The first group contains task-level metrics: `avg_accuracy`, `avg_forgetting`, and `backward_transfer`. The second contains geometry-level metrics: final and stage-wise transport gap, transport loss, routing-stability loss, and mean absolute excess. The third contains controller-state metrics: split counts, merge counts, reset counts, adaptation steps, and final hyperparameters. Without this three-level reporting, operator ablations are much less persuasive because they cannot show *how* a local operator change propagates into continual-learning behavior.

A particularly strong version of this section would also distinguish **already executable** ablations from **near-term code-exposed** ablations. Already executable studies include transport-weight on/off, lifecycle-threshold suppression, merge disablement through `prototype_merge_usage_threshold=0`, relocation disablement through `prototype_relocation_strength=0`, and adaptation strategy comparisons. Near-term but not yet fully script-exposed studies include `sinkhorn_topk` versus `kl_topk`, prototype-count scaling beyond one prototype per task, and explicit prior-strength/capacity-blend sweeps under a fixed routing strategy. Writing the section this way keeps the paper honest while making the future experimental roadmap very concrete.

Finally, the paper should state the expected failure modes revealed by these operator ablations. If a method needs every operator switched on to look competitive, then its practical value is weaker. If, however, the ablation shows a coherent pattern—for example, Sinkhorn improves balance-related diagnostics, transport loss improves barycenter alignment, split reduces overload, merge recycles redundant capacity, and adaptation improves closed-loop recovery—then the overall continual sparse-routing story becomes much stronger. This is exactly the kind of evidence needed to support even a modest theoretical contribution claim.

## Discussion and Broader Impact Draft

The most defensible interpretation of Continual ASAM is that it shifts continual learning from parameter-space regularization alone toward **routing-space control**. This is the main conceptual value of the framework. Instead of asking only whether parameters drift after each task, the method asks whether the model can maintain a stable and diagnosable sparse-routing geometry while still admitting enough flexibility to incorporate new tasks. From that perspective, the contribution is not just another anti-forgetting penalty, but a way of making interference, overload, redundancy, and route drift visible through explicit controller variables.

This perspective is potentially useful even beyond the particular benchmark scale used here. In resource-constrained or continually updated settings, full retraining is often too expensive, while fully dense interaction patterns make it difficult to localize why old knowledge is being overwritten. A prototype-routed sparse controller offers a middle ground: it can preserve the computational motivation of sparse attention while adding a modular memory geometry that can be monitored and adjusted over time. If future large-scale experiments support the same pattern, this could be relevant for domain-incremental language systems, adaptive retrieval modules, online robotics policies, or edge-deployed models that must absorb small updates without global retraining.

At the same time, the broader impact should be discussed with caution. Continual-routing systems explicitly partition traffic through learned prototypes, and that partitioning may reflect or amplify biases already present in the data stream. Rare or minority patterns may receive too little capacity, be repeatedly merged into dominant prototypes, or be reset more often if usage-based criteria are interpreted too naively. In application settings that involve people, domains, or user groups, this creates a real fairness concern: a controller that appears stable in aggregate may still underserve infrequent but important subpopulations.

There is also a deployment risk related to interpretability. Exported diagnostics such as transport gap, excess, and routing stability are useful, but they are still surrogates rather than direct guarantees of robust behavior. A practitioner could easily over-trust these signals and assume that low transport loss implies safe adaptation in all cases. The paper should therefore emphasize that controller diagnostics are decision aids, not formal safety certificates.

From a systems perspective, a second trade-off concerns complexity. Continual ASAM adds routing state, lifecycle heuristics, and adaptation logic beyond a standard sparse-attention baseline. This extra structure may improve retention and interpretability, but it also increases tuning burden and implementation surface area. Broader impact claims should therefore be framed around settings where controlled adaptation and diagnosis genuinely matter, rather than implying that every sparse-attention application should adopt a continual controller by default.

A positive broader-impact angle is that transport-aware continual routing may enable more efficient model maintenance. If a system can preserve prior capability with smaller updates, less replay, or less full retraining, it may reduce compute costs and shorten deployment cycles for evolving models. That said, such sustainability claims should remain conditional until they are backed by direct efficiency measurements at larger scale.

Overall, the discussion should return to one core point: Continual ASAM is most compelling not because it solves continual learning in full generality, but because it provides a structured and inspectable way to couple sparse attention with continual-routing control. If the empirical evidence continues to support that view, the broader contribution may be less about a single new operator and more about a design principle for future modular adaptive systems.

## Conservative Novelty Statement

If this draft is turned into a paper, the safest novelty statement is:

> We propose a continual-learning extension of adaptive sparse attention in which prototype routing, capacity-constrained optimal transport, and transport-aware lifecycle control are unified into a single sparse-attention framework. Our contribution is primarily the formulation and integration of these mechanisms into a coherent continual sparse-routing system with explicit diagnostic and control variables, rather than the invention of each component in isolation.

## Stronger but Still Defensible Statement

> To our knowledge, the combination of prototype-routed sparse attention, Sinkhorn capacity targets, transport-excess lifecycle control, and stage-wise continual-learning diagnostics has not been presented as a single runnable ASAM-style continual-learning system.
